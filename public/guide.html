Inkwell The guide

A writing studio that runs on your machine.

Inkwell plans, drafts, edits and typesets a novel using a model you downloaded and own. No API keys. No per-word billing. No account. Your manuscript is a folder of Markdown on your own disk, and nothing about it ever leaves the building.

The deal

It's free because charging you would have made it worse

Every hosted writing tool has the same quiet problem: the thing you want (more drafts, longer context, another pass over chapter nine) is the thing that costs the vendor money. So the product grows a meter. You start rationing. You stop asking for the fifth revision that would have been the good one.

Inkwell removes the meter by removing the vendor. The model runs on hardware you already paid for, so the marginal cost of one more attempt is a few seconds of fan noise. That single decision is what makes the rest of the design possible — the interview can ask twenty questions, the reviser can try again and again, the whole book can be re-outlined on a whim, because none of it appears on a bill.

The best feature of a local model isn't privacy. It's that you stop counting.

Nothing to sign up for

No key, no login, no seat. Clone it, npm start, done. There's no server of mine to go down or get bought.

Nothing phones home

No telemetry, no analytics, no crash reporting. Unplug the network and the whole studio still works.

Nothing is locked in

Your book is folders of Markdown and JSON. Open it in any editor, put it in git, back it up by copying it.

The trade is honest: you supply the compute. A laptop from the last few years will run a model good enough to be genuinely useful, and a desktop with a real GPU will run one that's better than useful. The setup section tells you exactly which to pull.

The shape of it

Three pieces, all on your desk

Inkwell isn't an AI product with a model bolted on. It's scaffolding around a model — prompts, context assembly, structure, and a UI that keeps you in charge of every word. Here's the whole system.

That's it. Inkwell never calls anything but Ollama, and Ollama never calls anything at all once the model is downloaded. If your firewall asked you to allow something, it was a phone on your own Wi-Fi.

★ The centrepiece

The interview is the whole trick

Ask any model to "write me a fantasy novel outline" and you'll get the same outline it gives everybody. Not because it's stupid — because you didn't tell it anything. The gap between a generic book and yours is a few hundred words of specifics that only exist in your head, and no prompt template gets them out.

So Inkwell doesn't start with a prompt. It starts with a developmental editor who interviews you, one question at a time, and writes each question from everything you've already said.

What it actually feels like

You type a premise. Inkwell asks one thing. You answer in whatever register you like — a sentence, a rant, a shrug. Then it proposes the next question and hands you two buttons: Ask this, or Skip and get a different one.

InkwellMaren forges maps that alter the real world. Before we talk about how that's discovered — what does she want badly enough to keep doing it after she knows?
YouShe's paying off her father's debt. He surveyed the coast honestly and it ruined him. She's making the money back the crooked way and telling herself it's a correction.
InkwellSo the forgeries are an argument with a dead man. Who's the first person to notice a map is wrong — and do they come to her, or go over her head?
YouA junior surveyor. He comes to her first, which is the mistake. She talks him out of it and it works, and that's the moment she stops being sympathetic.
Suggested next question · probing: the midpoint reversal
You've made her complicit on purpose. What does the junior surveyor do six weeks later that she can't talk her way out of?
Ask thisSkip

Notice what the model is doing there. It named the thing you'd implied but not said — "an argument with a dead man" — and then aimed the next question at the structural hole your answer just opened. That's not a template. It couldn't have asked it of anybody else's book.

Thin answers make thin books

This is the one place where effort pays compound interest. The interview is upstream of the outline, which is upstream of the bible, which is upstream of every sentence the model will ever write for you. Here's the same question answered two ways, and what came out the far end.

Thin answer
"There's a confrontation near the end and she has to choose."
What the outline gave back Chapter 14 — The Confrontation. Maren faces the consequences of her actions and must decide who she really is.
Thick answer
"He brings the honest survey to her kitchen, not the guild. She can burn it or file it. Burning it saves her and finishes what her father started — the coast stays wrong forever. She burns it, and the book doesn't forgive her."
What the outline gave back Chapter 14 — The Kitchen Table. Tam lays the true survey between the salt and the lamp and waits. Maren argues it's already too late to matter. She feeds it to the stove page by page while he watches, and neither of them says anything afterwards.

Same model. Same settings. The difference is entirely upstream. Specifics in, specifics out — objects, rooms, who's standing where, what it costs. Abstractions in ("she must confront her past") and you get the abstraction back with better adjectives.

What it's fishing for

The interviewer is steered toward the load-bearing parts of a story and asks about whichever is currently weakest. You'll see it circle these:

Want vs. need The inciting incident The antagonistic force The midpoint reversal The climax What the ending costs Tone Setting

You don't have to know these answers before you start — that's rather the point. Half of them you'll invent in the act of answering, which is the oldest trick in writing and the reason talking to someone about your book works at all.

It waits for you

Leave mid-sentence

The transcript is written to plan.json after every exchange. Close the tab, come back next week, hit Resume. Nothing is held in a session.

Answer more later

Kept answering after you built the outline? A gold bar appears: "you've answered 3 more questions since this outline was built." One click folds them in.

Then it builds the book's spine

When you've said enough, Build outline & bible reads the whole transcript twice — once to design a four-act, 12–20 chapter outline with beats, once to extract the story bible (characters, locations, themes, tone, style). The prompt is explicit that the premise sets the world, but the author's answers are what the book is actually about. If your premise is high-concept and your answers are about two people in a kitchen, you get the book about the kitchen.

And it keeps paying out. Refine structure lets you critique the outline in plain English — "the middle act is rushed, split it"; "move the reveal earlier"; "aim for 18 chapters" — and shows you a before/after with new, moved and kept chapters colour-coded before anything changes. Chapters you've already written are matched by title and preserved. The interview is never spent.

The engine room

Why it doesn't forget chapter three

The failure mode of AI prose is amnesia. Twenty pages in, the model has lost the thread: eye colours drift, a dead character walks in, the voice flattens toward the mean of the internet. It isn't a mystery — the model only sees what you put in the prompt, and naïve tools put in the last few paragraphs.

Inkwell assembles three scopes of memory on every single generation:

After a chapter is finished, Inkwell folds it into a running story so far summary, so the top layer stays current without ever resending the whole manuscript. All three layers are adjustable in Settings — if generation feels slow, that's the first place to look, because everything there is sent on every request.

Two honest ways to use it

Your editor, or your engine

People arrive at a tool like this from opposite directions, and Inkwell refuses to take a side. Both of these are supported all the way down; most writers slide between them by the hour.

The model as your editor

You write every word. It reads, questions and tightens.

Interview yourself into a real structure. Draft by hand. Then select a passage, write actual editorial notes on it — "cut the exposition, make her anger cold not loud, end on the door closing" — and read the revision as a diff. Take the two changes that were right; throw the rest away. It's a tireless reader who has memorised your story bible and never gets bored of chapter four.

The model as your engine

It drafts. You steer, cut and decide.

Build the outline, then let Continue writing lay down a first pass against the chapter's beats while you sit on the brake — direction in the instruction box, Describe / set the scene when a room needs to exist, quick rewrites on anything limp. Nobody has to know how the sausage was made, and the bible keeps the sausage consistent.

Either way, the model proposes and you dispose. That's not a slogan — it's how the code works.

There's also the third mode nobody admits to: using it to get unstuck. Ask for a description of a room you can't picture, read it, hate it, and discover you knew exactly what the room looked like all along. That counts. Delete the suggestion and keep the unlock.

The dials

Controlling the creativity

"Creativity" in a language model is mostly one thing: how far down the list of plausible next words it's willing to reach. Low, and it picks the most likely word every time — coherent, competent, faintly dull. High, and it starts choosing the fourth-most-likely word, which is where both the good images and the nonsense live.

Temperature 0.80

Drag it. Same chapter, same beat, same bible — different appetite for risk.

0.2 · flat0.6 · careful0.9 · lively1.3 · feverish
0.8 — lively The rain came off the estuary in sheets, and Maren counted the roads she had invented, one for every year she had been lying to the guild.

Illustrative — hand-written to show what the dial changes, not four real generations.

Most prose wants 0.7–0.9. Below 0.5 it stops taking any risk at all; above 1.1 you'll spend more time cutting than you save. Two neighbours worth knowing: top-p narrows the pool of words it's allowed to sample from at all (0.9 is a good default, and it's a safer knob than temperature), and repeat penalty stops the model falling in love with a phrase — nudge it up if you keep seeing the same image twice a page.

The other kind of control

Sampling dials are the crude instrument. The precise ones are all about what the model is told, and they live in Settings and in your story bible.

DialWhat it really doesTurn it when
Surrounding prose
100–1200 words		 How much of your actual writing goes in as the immediate voice reference. Raise it if the voice drifts mid-scene. Lower it if generation is slow.
Send the story bible Characters, locations and lore go in on every request. Leave it on. Turn it off only for a scratch scene with no continuity.
Send the running summary The "story so far" travels with each generation. Leave it on past chapter three. It's what stops contradictions.
Style guide (in the bible) A sentence or two describing the voice to hold. Goes into the system prompt. Always. This is the highest-leverage text box in the app.

Nothing enters your manuscript without you

This is the part that matters most and gets talked about least. Inkwell's AI actions never write to your chapter file. They produce a proposal, rendered as a word-level diff you read like a code review, with four buttons: Accept, More notes, Retry, Discard.

And when a revision is nearly right, More notes ↻ keeps the original, its last attempt and your new feedback all in view at once, so the second pass is a real second pass rather than a fresh roll of the dice. That loop — notes, diff, more notes — is the closest thing here to actual editing, and it's free to run twenty times.

The payoff

Then you get to see the book

The Pages tab lays your chapters out as printed pages at a real trim size. Not a preview skin — the browser's own layout engine paginates into columns exactly the width and height of the text block, so each column is a page. Line breaks, hyphens, justification, drop caps and the way text flows around a picture are the genuine article.

Everything on that page is a control: trim size from pocket to letter, mirrored inner and outer margins, typeface, point size, leading, justification and hyphenation, drop caps or small caps, chapter-opener style and numbering, running heads, folios, scene-break ornaments, paper tint and ink. Six named looks to start from — and if you'd rather not fiddle, let the model design it: the same local model reads your genre and tone and sets the page to match, with a sentence explaining why.

Art that text flows around

Drop in an image, pin it to a paragraph, and choose the wrap: box, circle, oval — or the artwork's own outline, traced from the transparent edges of a PNG.

Print at the real trim

Print → Save as PDF gives you a 6×9 PDF that's 6×9, with your type and your art where you put them.

EPUB that keeps the design

Export carries the typography, chapter openers, drop caps, scene breaks and placed art. Uploaded fonts get embedded. Straight to KDP.

Setup

Getting Ollama and a model running

Ollama is the piece that actually runs the model. It's one download, it has no account, and once it's installed it sits quietly in the background listening on localhost:11434. Inkwell talks to it and nothing else.

  1. Install Ollama

    Grab it from ollama.com/download — there's a normal installer for macOS and Windows. On Linux:

    # installs and starts the background service
curl -fsSL https://ollama.com/install.sh | sh

    Check it's alive. This should print a version, not an error:

    ollama --version

  2. Pull a model

    This is the one real decision. A model's size is roughly its appetite for RAM — as a rule of thumb a quantized model needs about 0.7 GB per billion parameters, plus room for context. Gemma is a good default family: small ones are quick and surprisingly literate, big ones plan properly. 

    # pick one to start — this is a few GB, so it takes a minute
ollama pull gemma3:4b      # light, fast, fine for trying the flow
ollama pull gemma3:12b     # the sweet spot on 16 GB
ollama pull llama3.1:8b    # solid all-rounder
ollama pull mistral-nemo   # 12B, lovely prose, long context

# what have I got?
ollama list

  3. Start Inkwell

    It prints where to find itself:

  4. Pick your model, watch the dot

    Top right of the app there's a dropdown listing everything ollama list knows about, and a dot beside it. Green means Ollama answered. Red means it isn't running — start it and reload. Your choice is remembered, and the gear icon lets you set a default for new books.

The two-model habit

You can change model between actions, and it's worth doing. The planning steps — interview, outline, bible, refine — run a handful of times and benefit enormously from a bigger model. Drafting runs constantly and benefits from speed. Plan with the 27B, draft with the 8B, switch in the dropdown whenever you like. Nothing is tied to a book.

When something goes wrong

What you seeWhat it meansFix
“The model x isn't installed in Ollama” You've selected a model you don't actually have — Inkwell catches this before it fails. Run the ollama pull it shows you, or pick an installed model from the dropdown.
“Ollama not running”, red dot Nothing is listening on 11434. Start the Ollama app, or ollama serve in a terminal.
Empty outline, or “returned an empty structure” The model was asked for strict JSON and produced something unparseable. Small models do this. Try a larger model, or answer one more interview question — richer input tends to produce cleaner output.
Everything is slow Too much context per request, or a model too big for the machine. In Settings, drop surrounding prose to ~200 words and lower the context window. Then try a smaller model.
Prose repeats itself Repeat penalty too low, or too little surrounding prose to anchor the voice. Nudge repeat penalty to ~1.15 and raise surrounding prose.
Ollama is on a different machine You want the model on the desktop and Inkwell on the laptop. Start Ollama with OLLAMA_HOST=0.0.0.0 ollama serve, then run Inkwell with OLLAMA_HOST=http://192.168.1.30:11434 npm start.

Elsewhere in the house

Writing from the sofa

Inkwell listens on every network interface, so the moment it's running on your desktop it's reachable from anything else on the same Wi-Fi. Open the second address it printed on your phone and you get the whole studio: the outline map drags with a finger, the AI panel is a bottom sheet, and the Pages view shows one page at a time — swipe to turn. Add it to your home screen and it opens full-screen like an app.

Only the machine running Inkwell needs Ollama. Your phone is just a window, and the manuscript never leaves the desktop.

There's no password. Anyone on your network can open your book. That's a deliberate trade for a personal tool on a home network — but on shared or public Wi-Fi, lock it down:

INKWELL_HOST=127.0.0.1 npm start

If the phone can't find it: check both devices are on the same network (not a guest network), and say yes when macOS or Windows asks whether to allow incoming connections.

Start here

Your first hour, in order

  1. Write one paragraph of premise

    Not a pitch — a seed. The one sentence that made you want to write this at all, plus enough setting to stand on. Create the book; the interview starts itself.

  2. Answer six questions properly

    Twenty minutes, real specifics, no shrugging. Skip any question that bores you — the next one will be better. This is the highest-value hour you'll spend in the app, and everything downstream inherits it.

  3. Build the outline and bible

    Read the chapter list. If the middle sags, say so in Refine structure and look at the before/after. Repeat until the spine looks like your book.

  4. Fix the tone and style guide by hand

    Open the Bible and rewrite those two boxes yourself. Two sentences. They're in the system prompt on every generation and they do more for the voice than any sampling setting.

  5. Write chapter one your way

    By hand with the model as editor, or by beats with the model as engine. Try the notes-and-diff loop once on a paragraph you're unhappy with — that's the moment the tool usually clicks.

  6. Look at it as a book

    Open Pages. Pick a trim, pick a face, watch your own sentence break across a real line. It's a cheap thrill and a surprisingly good motivator.

Ownership

Where your work actually lives

No database, no bundle, no proprietary anything. One folder per book, all of it readable with any text editor twenty years from now.

Inkwell/books/<book-id>/ book.json title, author, premise, POV, tense bible.json characters, locations, themes, tone, style, running summary outline.json acts, chapters, beats, map positions plan.json the interview transcript — this is the good stuff notes.md freeform scratch space design.json trim, margins, typography, page furniture art.json where each picture sits in the prose assets/ images and fonts you've uploaded chapters/ *.md — your actual prose, one file per chapter

Put that folder in git and you have version history for your novel. Copy it to a drive and you have a backup. Move it to another machine and everything — outline, bible, interview, page design — comes with it.

The tool should be the disposable part. The folder is the book.
